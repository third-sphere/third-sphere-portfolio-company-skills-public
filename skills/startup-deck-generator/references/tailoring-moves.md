# Tailoring moves

How to turn what you learned about a target into a specific set of changes. The
framework is deliberately blunt — four verbs — because the value is in the reasoning
attached to each call, not in the taxonomy.

## The four moves

For every slide in the base deck, one of:

| Move | When | What to write down |
|---|---|---|
| **Keep** | Already right for this audience | Nothing. Silence means keep. |
| **Modify** | Right slide, wrong emphasis, order, framing, or headline | The specific change, and the signal that prompted it |
| **Remove** | Doesn't serve this audience's decision, or actively hurts | Why, so a future reader doesn't restore it by accident |
| **Add** | This archetype needs something the deck lacks | The slide, and where it goes in the order |

Two disciplines make the output usable rather than decorative.

**Cite the signal.** Every modify, remove, and add should name the thing that
justified it — a thesis post, a portfolio pattern, a partner's stated view, something
the founder heard on a first call. A change with no cited signal is a preference
dressed as strategy, and the founder can't evaluate it. When the signal is thin, say
that too: "weak signal, worth doing anyway because it costs nothing" is an honest and
useful note.

**Keep the diff small.** A tailored deck that changes twelve slides isn't tailored,
it's a different deck — and it usually means the base deck was wrong for everyone,
which is a `pitch-content-guide` problem rather than this one. Three to six changes
plus one added slide is the normal shape; a fund that is genuinely two archetypes
normally justifies two adds, one for each. If the list runs much longer than that, say
so plainly — that finding is worth more to the founder than the changes.

## Slide order comes from elsewhere

Do not invent a slide order here — restating one in a second place is how two skills
quietly start contradicting each other. Take the baseline from the first available
source, and say which one you used:

1. **The founder's existing deck.** Their order is the baseline; a diff needs nothing
   else. This is the normal case.
2. **The `pitch-content-guide` skill's stage reference,** if that skill is installed.
   Canonical for the base order at each stage.
3. **Neither** — no deck, no reference. That's a content-design problem, not a
   tailoring one; hand off rather than improvising an order.

This file governs the *diff*. The per-archetype addition lives in
`investor-archetypes.md` under "the slide a standard deck lacks."

## Active risks — check these first

Content that can actively hurt with a particular audience, as distinct from content
that's merely suboptimal. Work through these before the slide-by-slide pass, because
a single one of them can undo an otherwise strong deck.

Each derives from the **archetype**, so it can be assessed from the base deck plus the
archetype section — no target-specific research required.

**TAM built top-down.** A number reached by taking a large market and multiplying by
a percentage. Lands badly everywhere and fatally with a sector specialist, who knows
the bottom-up figure. Fix: rebuild as units × price × reachable share, or cut the
slide. A deck with no market slide beats one with a market slide the audience
disbelieves, because disbelief doesn't stay contained to that slide.

**Undefined metrics.** "ARR," "customers," "pipeline," "users" without a stated
definition. A careful investor discounts an undefined number rather than guessing
generously. Fix: define it on the slide, in small type if need be. This is also where
the honesty line lives — see `honest-tailoring.md`, because the temptation to define
a metric differently per audience is exactly what that file exists to prevent.

**"No competitors."** Reads as either incuriosity or evasion, never as whitespace. A
specialist will name three before you finish the sentence. Fix: name the real
alternatives, including "the customer does nothing" and internal spreadsheets, then
say why you win.

**Wrong-stage framing.** A pre-seed argument in a Series A deck (all vision, no unit
economics) or the reverse (a spreadsheet where an insight should be). Fix: re-anchor to
the stage the numbers actually support — which is not always the stage the round is
named after — using whichever base-order source you established above.

**Hardware timelines in software framing.** Deep-tech and hardware companies pitched
with SaaS-shaped growth curves. A generalist may not notice; a specialist reads it as
not understanding your own business. Fix: show the cost or capability curve and the
capital required to walk it, and let the growth curve be the shape it really is.

**A vague ask.** "Raising $3M to accelerate growth." Every archetype punishes this,
and it's the cheapest thing in the deck to fix. Fix: what the round buys, what will be
true when it's spent, and — for a seed VC especially — the round construction (see
that archetype's section).

**Ceiling visible in the framing.** Only matters for multistage funds, but matters a
lot there: a deck whose most ambitious claim is a modest one is a pass from a firm
underwriting option value. Fix: state the large-outcome path explicitly rather than
leaving it implied. Note this is a framing change, never a numbers change.

## Modifications that are almost always worth making

Cheap, low-risk, and applicable across archetypes.

**Reorder so their filter is satisfied early.** Whatever the archetype decides on —
ownership for a seed fund, outcome size for a multistage, domain rigour for a
specialist, strategic fit for a CVC — should be addressed in the first third, not
saved for the end. Decks are frequently read for ninety seconds.

**Adopt their vocabulary.** If a fund's thesis writing consistently says "industrial
decarbonization" and the deck says "climate tech," change the deck. This costs
nothing and signals that the founder read their material — which, more than the
vocabulary itself, is what registers.

**Lead the proof point they'd care about most.** Companies usually have three or four
credible proof points and default to leading with the largest. The one that matters
is the one this audience's thesis is about, which is often not the largest.

**Name the objection you know they'll raise.** Where the archetype's "what kills it"
list intersects a genuine weakness, address it on-slide rather than hoping it doesn't
come up. Pre-empting an objection converts it from a discovery into a demonstration
of self-awareness. This is the single highest-leverage add in most tailoring passes.

## Presenting the diff

Show the founder the gap analysis before building anything. It is the cheapest place
to catch a wrong read of the target, and founders frequently know something about the
fund that no amount of public research surfaces — a prior conversation, a friend at
the firm, a pass two years ago.

Structure it as a table (slide, move, change, signal) followed by the active risks
found and any structural observation about the deck as a whole. Then stop and wait.
Building before this checkpoint means rebuilding after it.
