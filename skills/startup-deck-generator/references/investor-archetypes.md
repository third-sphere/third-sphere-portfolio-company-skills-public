# Investor archetypes

Four archetypes, because they are four different buyers. The same company, same
numbers, same slides in a different order will land very differently across them —
not because one is smarter, but because they are underwriting different questions.

Read only the section for the target. Reading all four wastes context and blurs the
distinctions that make the tailoring worth doing.

## Which one is this?

Ask the founder rather than inferring. The tell that inference is unreliable: a
$200M fund with "seed" in its materials and a sector thesis on its homepage could be
any of three of these, and the difference decides which slide leads.

If the founder isn't sure, these questions usually settle it fast: Does the fund
lead rounds and take a target ownership? Does it have a fund large enough to follow
on into a Series A itself? Does it publish domain research? Is there a corporate
parent whose business unit could buy from you?

## Seed VC

**What they optimize for: ownership at entry.** A dedicated seed fund returns from a
handful of companies and cannot buy up meaningfully later — so entry ownership is the
lever they actually control. This has a consequence founders routinely miss: they
underwrite *round construction* nearly as hard as company quality. A great company
in an unleadable round is a real pass.

**Check and ownership.** Roughly $1–4M, targeting 10–20%, usually leading or
co-leading, often with a board seat or observer right.

**Decision process.** A sponsoring partner carries it to a partnership meeting; two
to six weeks. One convinced partner can move a lot, which makes the individual
partner's prior interests unusually predictive. References tend to come late, after
internal conviction.

**The slide a standard deck lacks: round construction.** How much is being raised,
how much is committed and by whom, what's left, and what ownership a lead gets at the
proposed price. Most decks put a round size on the ask slide and stop. Seed funds are
doing this arithmetic anyway — doing it for them, accurately, reads as competence and
removes the most common reason a seed fund stalls.

**What kills it.** No clear ownership available at a price that works. A party round
with no lead and no one willing to become one. A founder who can't say what the round
buys. Traction that may well be real but is undefined — if "customer" isn't defined,
a careful seed investor discounts the number to zero rather than guessing.

**Reading their public signals.** The portfolio page is the thesis, stated more
honestly than the copy above it. Pull the last twelve to eighteen months of announced
deals specifically: stated stage and actual recent stage diverge often, and the
recent behaviour is what governs. Partner writing and podcast appearances are where
the real filter gets described.

## Multistage / platform VC

**What they optimize for: option value on the next round.** A seed check from a
multistage firm is partly a call option — they can fund the Series A themselves. So
the question behind every question is not "is this a good seed investment" but "could
this become a company we'd want to own much more of." That reframing explains most of
their otherwise-puzzling behaviour.

**Check and ownership.** Wide range, roughly $500K–$5M at seed. Markedly less
ownership-sensitive than a seed fund, frequently no board seat early.

**Decision process.** Fast to interest, slower to conviction. Often sourced by an
associate or principal before a partner engages, so early enthusiasm is a weaker
signal here than elsewhere. The platform team — recruiting, go-to-market, data — is
usually part of what they're selling and worth acknowledging.

**The slide a standard deck lacks: the path to a very large outcome.** State
explicitly what has to be true for this to become a category-defining company, and
name the Series A milestones. The second half matters more than founders expect: a
multistage investor is underwriting their own next decision, so showing them the
milestone set they'd be judging in eighteen months is doing their work for them.

**What kills it.** A good business with a visible ceiling. "Solid $100M company" is
a pass here and a yes at a seed fund — same company, different fund math. Also be
ready for signalling risk to come up from their side, because they know founders
worry about it: have an answer for what happens if they don't do the A.

**Reading their public signals.** Distinguish seed deals they *led* from ones they
followed — the ratio tells you which product you're being sold. Then check whether
their own seed companies graduate to their own Series A; a firm that rarely follows
its own seeds is selling an option it doesn't often exercise, which is worth knowing
before you price the relationship.

## Sector specialist

**What they optimize for: domain truth.** They have seen thirty companies in this
exact space and on several dimensions they know the terrain better than the founder —
incumbent behaviour, real unit economics, regulatory timelines, why the last three
attempts failed. This makes them the most rewarding audience for a strong company and
the least forgiving for a loose one.

**Check and ownership.** Varies widely. Often ownership-sensitive like a seed fund,
but with more conviction range and more appetite to follow on.

**Decision process.** Deep technical diligence, frequently with in-house or network
experts. Slower and more thorough, and more likely to end in either a real yes or a
fast, well-reasoned no. Both are more useful than a slow maybe.

**The slide a standard deck lacks: the domain-specific proof slide.** What it is
depends on the domain — cost-per-unit trajectory and bill-of-materials path for
hardware and climate; reimbursement and coding pathway for health; licensing and
regulatory posture for fintech; data rights and model provenance for AI. Generic
decks omit this slide, and a specialist reads the omission itself as information
about whether the founder knows what the hard part is.

**What kills it.** Overclaiming on domain fundamentals — the one audience guaranteed
to catch it. Benchmarking against the wrong incumbent. A top-down TAM when they
already know the bottom-up number. Not knowing a competitor they consider obvious,
which is less a knowledge gap than a signal about how the founder does research.

**Reading their public signals.** Published theses and research are unusually
load-bearing here: specialists write to attract dealflow, so the writing describes
the exact frame they'll evaluate you in. Conference talks are the same material with
the hedges removed. Worth noting what they've *stopped* doing — an abandoned
sub-sector usually means a thesis they now consider disproven, and walking into it
without acknowledgement is an avoidable stumble.

## Corporate & strategic VC

**What they optimize for: strategic value to the parent,** with financial return as a
constraint rather than the objective. The consequence that governs everything else:
the real decision-maker is usually a business unit, not the investment team you're
talking to.

**Check and ownership.** Roughly $500K–$5M and up. Rarely leads. Often relatively
price-insensitive, and often wants information rights, occasionally with commercial
terms attached.

**Decision process.** The slowest of the four — investment team, then a business-unit
sponsor, then legal and procurement. Three to six months is ordinary. A champion
inside the business unit matters more than enthusiasm from the fund team, so identify
who that would be early.

**The slide a standard deck lacks: the partnership slide** — what a commercial
relationship with the parent looks like and what it's worth to them, kept visibly
*separate* from the investment ask. Keeping them separate is the whole craft here.
Bundling them invites their legal team to treat the investment as consideration for a
commercial agreement, which slows everything down and can kill both.

**What kills it.** Appearing to need them — dependency reads as risk to their own
counsel, not as commitment. A story that implies disrupting the parent's core
business, unless you have a sponsor who wants exactly that. Assuming exclusivity is
on offer. And asking for investment and a commercial deal in one breath, per above.

**Reading their public signals.** The parent's strategy statements and earnings calls
tell you more than the CVC's own website, because the fund's mandate is downstream of
them. Their prior investments answer the practical questions: do they lead, do they
take board seats, and did portfolio companies actually get commercial deals — that
last one being the thing most often promised and least often delivered.

## When a fund is two archetypes

Common and not a problem. A climate-focused seed fund is a sector specialist *and* a
seed VC; a corporate fund with real domain depth is a specialist *and* a CVC.

Resolve it by asking which one governs the **decision**, not which describes the
fund. The seed fund's ownership math is a hard constraint — if the round can't
deliver it, domain excellence won't rescue the deal. The specialist's domain rigour
is a hard filter — if the cost curve doesn't hold, ownership availability is
irrelevant. Lead with whichever constraint fails first, and satisfy the other in the
body of the deck.

## The partner often matters more than the fund

Firms have theses; partners have track records, and the partner is who champions the
deal in the room. Where the two diverge, weight the partner — a fund whose site says
"software" but whose target partner has done four hardware deals in two years is a
hardware conversation.

Worth an explicit look at: their prior operating background, what they've written or
said publicly, which boards they sit on, and what they've invested in over the last
year or two. The last one is the most predictive and the most often skipped.
