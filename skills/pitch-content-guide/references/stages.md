# Stage reference

Each stage is a different argument, not the same argument with bigger numbers. Read
the relevant section before drafting; the slide order below is the default and
should be varied when the company's specific strength warrants it.

The through-line across every stage: **sequence sober before exciting.** Establish
that the near-term is real, then let the long-term land. Leading with the big market
number before the reader believes the near-term is the single most common structural
mistake, and it reads as compensation.

## Pre-seed

**The argument:** this problem is real, urgent, and we are unusually equipped to
attack it.

There is no traction to speak of, so the weight lands on problem insight and team.
Investors are underwriting the founders' understanding of the problem more than any
evidence the solution works.

**Slide order:** Title · Problem · Insight (what you know others don't) · Solution ·
Why now · Team · Early validation · Market · Plan and milestones · Ask

**Emphasis.** The insight slide is the deck. It answers "why hasn't this been done?"
and it is where a pre-seed investor decides. Team comes early — unusually early
relative to later stages — because at pre-seed the team *is* the asset.

**Traps.** Don't fake traction; a waitlist presented as revenue reads as a
character flag. Don't over-specify the financial model — nobody believes month 34.
Do be specific about what the round buys and what will be true when it's spent.

## Seed

**The argument:** we have early evidence this works, and capital turns it into a
repeatable machine.

**Slide order:** Title · Problem · Solution · Why now · Traction · Business model ·
Market · Competition · Team · Go-to-market · Ask

**Emphasis.** Traction moves ahead of market — it is the slide investors flip to
first. Show trajectory rather than a snapshot: "X to Y in Z months" is an argument,
a single number is a fact. Pre-revenue, validated demand counts: LOIs, paid pilots,
design wins, letters from named customers.

**Traps.** Unsourced TAM. Claiming no competitors. A market slide before the reader
believes the traction.

For the full seven-deliverable seed system — memo, TEA, model, both decks, emails,
investor list — use `seed-pitch-kit`. This reference covers only deck content.

## Series A

**The argument:** the machine works and the unit economics prove it scales.

**Slide order:** Title · Problem · Solution · Traction · Unit economics · Market ·
Competition · Product roadmap · Team · Go-to-market · Financials · Ask

**Emphasis.** Unit economics become load-bearing: CAC, payback, LTV/CAC, gross
margin trajectory, net revenue retention. Cohort data if it exists. A Series A
investor is underwriting repeatability, so anything that shows the second cohort
behaving like the first is worth more than a bigger topline.

**Traps.** Margin claims that depend on an unshipped cost reduction — pre-answer
"what if it ships late?" Blended CAC hiding a paid-acquisition problem. Retention
mentioned but never quantified.

## Series B and later

**The argument:** category leadership is achievable and we are the ones achieving it.

**Slide order:** Title · Market position · Traction and financials · Unit economics
at scale · Expansion vectors · Competitive moat · Team and org · Financial plan ·
Ask

**Emphasis.** Shifts from "does this work" to "how big can this get and who else
could take it." Expansion — new segments, geographies, products — carries real
weight. Multi-year financials expected, with actuals not just projections.

## Growth equity / minority recap

**The argument:** this already works without outside capital; capital makes it
faster.

This is the stage most often got wrong, because the standard venture arc doesn't
fit. A profitable, capital-efficient company is not asking to be believed in — it is
offering a proven machine and asking for fuel. The pitch is fundamentally stronger
than a venture pitch and should be structured to say so.

**Slide order:** Title · Problem · Solution · Platform/product · Customer proof ·
**Financial profile** · Business model · Moat · Why now · Market · Impact (if
material) · Team · Path to <target> · Ask

**Emphasis.**

- **Financial profile comes early**, around slide 6. It's the strongest fact and it
  earns everything after it. Revenue *and* margin trajectory — margin expansion is
  usually the more impressive line and the more commonly buried one.
- **Capital efficiency is the headline, not a footnote.** "Twelve years profitable
  on under $1M raised" is a rarer claim than any growth rate. If it's spread across
  three slides, consider making it the spine.
- **Retention and tenure** matter more than at any earlier stage, because the moat
  argument depends on them. Chase these numbers hard; a growth investor will ask
  within the first ten minutes and their absence is conspicuous.
- **Use of proceeds must be the company's, not yours.** A first-time raiser often
  hasn't decided. Flag it rather than reconstructing plausible buckets from
  positioning — an inferred plan that the founders then contradict in the meeting is
  worse than an honest gap.

**Traps.** Applying venture framing (TEA curves, hockey sticks, hypergrowth
projections) to a company whose whole story is disciplined compounding. Two years of
financials when twelve exist — the long history is the asset, so get more of it.
Treating the first outside round as a weakness rather than a choice.

## Secondary and founder liquidity

**The argument:** the founders have earned liquidity and the business continues
without disruption.

Rarely a standalone deck; usually a growth deck with a modified ask and two extra
concerns addressed.

**Emphasis.** Continuity — who stays, what changes, what governance looks like
after. Investors' real question is whether the people who built it remain motivated.
Address it directly rather than letting it sit unspoken. If the founders are taking
partial liquidity and staying, say what proportion and why that structure was chosen.

**Traps.** Ambiguity about primary versus secondary split. Vague post-close
governance. Any suggestion the founders are checking out.

## Choosing when the company straddles two stages

Companies frequently don't match a label — a Series A-sized company that never
raised a seed, a profitable business raising its first institutional round. When in
doubt, structure to **the argument the numbers support**, not the round's name.

The practical test: which single fact is most likely to make an investor lean in?
Build the arc so the deck earns the right to state that fact, then states it with
force. If the strongest fact is profitability, that's a growth arc regardless of
what the round is called. If it's a step-change in the last two quarters, that's a
Series A arc even if the company is eight years old.
