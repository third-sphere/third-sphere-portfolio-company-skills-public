# Slide patterns

Treatments for the slides that recur at every stage. Each includes the failure mode,
because knowing how a slide usually goes wrong is more useful than knowing how it
should look.

## Stat callouts

A very large number (60–90pt) over a small label. The most scannable format that
exists, and the right default for problem, traction, and unit-economics slides.

Three per slide is the sweet spot. Four is tolerable. Five is a table pretending to
be a callout.

**Failure mode:** using a non-numeric symbol (∞, ?, ∅) as one of the callouts to
make a rhetorical point. It reads as glib in an otherwise rigorous deck and
undermines the numbers beside it. If you can't fill the slot with a real figure,
use two callouts.

## Traction

Show **trajectory, not snapshot.** "X to Y in Z months" is an argument; "Y" is a
fact. Two data points make a line and are the minimum; five make a trend and are
much harder to argue with. Chase the longer series — it is usually available and
almost always underused.

**Logos.** If the company has strong customer logos and clearance to use them, a
clean grid works. Without clearance, or where logo files don't exist, a **styled
name wall** — muted all-caps type in a grid, with the single best-known name
elevated into a distinct callout card — reads as more confident than a grid of
mismatched raster logos. Lead with the strongest name.

**Failure mode:** presenting logos the company has no permission to name. Public
display on a website is not clearance for an investor deck. Flag it every time; this
is routinely the highest-risk slide in a deck and the one people think least about.

## Competition

A comparison table or a positioning matrix. Tables land faster and survive being
read on a phone.

Structure the table so the company's column is visually elevated and every row is a
capability where the answer differs. Rows where everyone scores the same are noise.

Never claim no competitors. "The alternative is a spreadsheet and three vendors" is
a competitor. Naming incumbents accurately signals you understand the market;
pretending they don't exist signals the opposite.

**Failure mode:** a matrix engineered so the company sits alone in the top-right.
Investors have seen hundreds and discount them automatically. A table with one row
where a competitor genuinely wins is far more credible than a clean sweep.

## Team

Connect each person to **the specific problem**, not just their credentials. "Ran
logistics for 400 facilities" beats "20 years of operations experience."

Address gaps directly and say how they'll be filled. A named gap with a plan reads
as self-awareness; an unmentioned gap that the investor spots reads as blindness.

**Failure mode:** at any stage past seed, omitting founder names and faces. It is
conspicuous, and it is especially conspicuous when the raise narrative depends on
the founders — first-time equity sale, founder liquidity, succession. If headshots
and bios don't exist, that is a **blocking** gap, not a nice-to-have.

## Business model

Show the mechanism, not just the pricing. If revenue comes from two sources, a
two-sided diagram makes it legible in a way a paragraph cannot.

Where margins improve, **show causation**: not "45% → 82%" but "automating the most
expensive step cuts per-unit cost from $450 to $160, driving margin from 46% to
72%." A margin improvement without a named driver reads as a hope.

If the driver hasn't shipped yet, pre-answer the obvious question: what if it's
late? The strongest version is "current margins already work; the milestone is
upside."

---

## The ask

State the amount, the structure, the milestone it funds, and the date. Vagueness
here reads as unresolved rather than flexible.

When terms genuinely aren't set, flag them visibly rather than writing something
plausible. An investor who acts on an invented number and later learns it was
invented has learned something much worse than "they hadn't decided yet."

Close with the vision line — but only after the ask. Ending on vision without the
ask leaves the meeting without a next step.

---

## Bottom banners

A single-line takeaway at the bottom of key slides. They ensure the argument lands
even on a skim, which is how most decks are actually read.

Write them as assertions, not summaries. "Competitors do the middle. We do the line."
beats "Overview of our end-to-end capabilities."

---

## Universal design rules

Applies at every stage:

- **One idea per slide.** Two ideas means two slides.
- **Headlines are assertions, not labels.** "Profitable at $37M" beats "Financial
  Overview." This single change does more for a deck than any visual choice.
- **24pt minimum.** Decks are read on phones.
- **No animations.** They break in email and in PDF.
- **Numbers stated flatly.** If the traction is strong, adornment reads as
  insecurity. Let it stand.
- **Assign a content type to every slide** — chart, diagram, image, or text —
  before writing it. Unassigned slides become bullet lists by default.
